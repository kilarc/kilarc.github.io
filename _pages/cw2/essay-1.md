*Kilho Choi*

Assume you are a worker in your 20's earning 3.09 million won a month. Someone says that if you pay about 50 million won more from now, you will receive about 20 million won more after retirement. Ah, you were already paying into this system: your income now supports the retirement of the generation before you, and later generations will support yours. Before, the promise to pay you a pension later was not clearly written in law, but now the state says it will write that promise into law. What do you think? This is a question given to Korean people in 2026\.

For a new member with a monthly income of 3.09 million won in 2026, total contributions increase by 54.14 million won, while total pension benefits increase by 21.69 million won (Kim, 2025). The reform also raises the contribution rate from 9% to 13%, fixes the nominal income replacement rate at 43%, and writes the state's duty to guarantee pension payment into law (National Assembly Budget Office, 2025).

Now, back to the first question. What answer did you give? In Korea, different answers came out, especially between generations. So, I want to look at this issue as one way to see general social conflict, looking at the conflict between generations caused by the National Pension from the young generation's view. You may wonder exactly what "young" and "old" generation mean. Do not worry; that is intended. Rather than seeing this only as a difference between two groups, I hope you see age as a linear variable. Even when I write "young generation," I leave its exact range to your judgment.

## Legal Perspective

First, let us look at the legal side for statistic: after the reform passed, 63% of people aged 18-29 and 58% in their 30's opposed it, compared with 50% in their 40's, 45% in their 50's, 36% in their 60's, and 24% among those 70 or older (KBS World, 2025). Does the opposition of the young generation not stand out?

There was also a clear benefit for young people-more exactly, for everyone. The revised law says the state will guarantee stable and continuous payment of the National Pension (National Assembly Budget Office, 2025). Simply, even if the fund runs out and the state has to take debt, the pension is still guaranteed. If young people still oppose the reform, their opposition cannot be explained only by fear of receiving nothing.

## Political Perspective

Now politics. The reform passed with 194 votes in favor, 40 against, and 43 abstentions (Yi, 2025). Yet 10 of 14 lawmakers in their 30's opposed it, about 71.4%. If lawmakers in their 30's and 40's are combined, 13 of 44 opposed it, compared with 27 of 256 among those 50 or older (Song, 2026).

Then another question: is this simply because pensions are more friendly to progressive politics? Party differences clearly existed: in the Democratic Party, 155 voted in favor and 3 against; in the People Power Party, 37 in favor and 26 against (People's Solidarity for Participatory Democracy, 2025). However, eight lawmakers in their 30's and 40's from three different parties later held a joint press conference, raising the burden on future generations and inequality between generations (Joo, 2025). This can be interpreted as generational differences having a bigger influence than party or political agenda, showing that young people's opposition cannot be reasonably explained only by political logic.

## Economic Perspective

Then, money itself-the most direct part, and something everyone reacts to sensitively. You may wonder why money has not appeared yet, but money is not the exact answer either.

The National Pension recorded an annual investment return of 18.82% in 2025, and by June 2026 its temporary return reached 27.22%, with the total fund at about 1,865.6 trillion won (National Pension Service Investment Management, n.d.). Even with these results, only 30.8% of people in their 20's and 25.3% in their 30's said they trusted the National Pension, while 55.8% in their 50's and 62.9% of those 60 or older said they trusted it (Kim, 2026). From this view, the economic side also does not explain the issue exactly.

## Moral Perspective

Then, if the answer is not fully found above, what explains it? I think the part that best explains this opposition is morality, exactly the difference in values between generations.

A study on welfare attitudes in Korea also found growing differences between birth generations, and that trust in government and ideas about fairness are related to attitudes toward welfare burden (Ha, 2026). In another survey of 2,000 workers, trust in the sustainability of the National Pension was 20.5% in the 20's, 15.5% in the 30's, 24.4% in the 40's, 33.3% in the 50's, and 47.1% in the 60's. Also, the share saying unfair distribution had not been solved was over 25% in the 20's and about one-quarter in the 30's, compared with 14.6% in the 50's and 10.8% among those 60 or older.

Young people worried more about losing control over their own pension, while older people worried more that taxpayers might pay the cost if investment losses happened (Jeon, 2025). So generations differed not only in support or opposition, but also in what they thought was important when judging the same pension issue.

## Conclusion

We looked at young people's opposition to National Pension reform from legal, political, economic, and moral views. Each factor affects the issue, but none alone fully explains the difference between generations, especially the usual political, legal, and economic views in this case.

Generations that lived in different times have different trust and expectations about the state, institutions, and their own future, and these differences make dissonance, and they accept the same reform differently.

So, when we look not only at this issue but also at social conflict, especially conflict between generations, we should also pay attention to the dissonance caused by differences in values and in how each generation sees the state and the future.
