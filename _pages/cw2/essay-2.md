*Junkyo Kim and Kilho Choi*

Shared bicycles and scooters[^1] have made urbanites’ lives more convenient. These personal mobilities help users to reach subway stations, workplaces, and shops without relying on a car. However, this convenience has rather produced a growing problem on Korean sidewalks. When people used shared mobility, many leave their devices wherever that happens to be. As a result, shared bicycles and scooters are left to block sidewalks, cross alleys and other public areas.

The problem of illegal parking is greater than an inconvenience. A parked shared bicycle in front of a crosswalk could force pedestrians into traffic, while a parked shared scooter on the sidewalk could cause them to trip over it. Especially at night, a fallen handlebar is difficult to recognize. One Seoul resident reported seeing improperly parked personal mobilities “about once a day” and falling several times while walking (Han & Kim, 2020, para. 1). Reporting such devices does not always solve the problem quickly. Although residents raised 141,031 complaints through Seoul’s reporting system in 2023, the city’s towing rate fell from 70 percent in 2021 to 44 percent in 2023, and some residents found the illegally parked devices still in the same place (Lee, 2024). This delay leaves the personal mobilities on routes and increases the chance of an accident.

This essay is based on the premise that fines, towing, and collection systems have not been enough to prevent accidents caused by illegally parked shared bicycles and scooters. These measures work before damage occurs, but they offer little once someone has been injured. Therefore, Korea should add a post-damage compensation system to existing preventive policies. Injured pedestrians should be able to claim their medical expenses from the responsible user or the shared-mobility operator. The system should also compensate for certain work-related losses, such as missing a business meeting because of a shared bicycle blocking a road.

## Existing Solutions and Their Limits
{: .cw2-apa-heading}

Local governments have tried to solve illegal parking in several ways. The most common responses have been to fine users or remove improperly parked personal mobilities. Seoul introduced a towing system in 2021\. Under this system, the devices left near subway stations, bus stops, crosswalks, or on sidewalks can be collected. Operators are charged a towing fee of 40,000 won and a storage fee of 700 won per thirty minutes (Moon, 2026).[^2] Seoul has also expanded priority towing zones and protections zones for children, seniors, and people with disabilities (Lee, 2024). More recently, the city has attempted to make operators take responsibility by assessing them based on complaints, accident records, relocation speed, and public satisfaction (Moon, 2026).

Yet the results leave room for improvements. Seoul received 141,031 complaints through its parking-violation reporting system in 2023, which was a 49.7 percent increase from 94,181 complaints in 2022 (Lee, 2024). The number shows that, rather than people’s becoming familiarized with the reporting system, many citizens still see parking the personal mobilities in undesignated zone as a dire problem.

Several reasons suggest why preventive measures have not worked well. First, fines may too low to effectively influence users’ behavior. If a user is in a hurry, just leaving a bicycle nearby may seem much easier than finding a designated parking area. Second, simply removing (or towing) shared bicycles and scooters parked in violation of the regulations does not help a victim severely injured by the devices. Third, it is impossible to have all the workers who are assigned to collect and park the shared devices search every street, alley, and subway entrance 24/7. Finally, responsibility is unclear. The rider, the operator, and sometimes the city may all be involved. When an accident occurs, an injured pedestrian may not know who should reimburse his or her medical expenses or how to make a claim.

A recent study also points out that Seoul’s complaint-based towing process is mainly reactive. In other words, the city usually acts after someone reports a problem, which means there can be a delay before the obstruction is addressed (Kim & Kim, 2026). This delay matters since a shared bicycle may block a sidewalk for hours before collected, and during that time a pedestrian could be a victim.

## The Need for Post-Damage Compensation and Related Procedures
{: .cw2-apa-heading}

Because prevention has not fully solved the problem, Korea needs a stronger system for dealing with harm after it occurs. This does not mean that fines and towing are useless. They should remain important. On the other hand, a fair policy needs to protect those who accidentally were damaged by an illegally-parked shared bicycle or scooter. The procedures for post-damage compensation needs to be simple, based on evidence and to be used by everyone.

### Procedure for Medical Expenses

When a pedestrian is injured because of an improperly parked personal mobilities, the first step is to document the accident. If the injured person receives medical treatment, he or she should keep all receipts and medical records, including the doctor’s prescriptions. The photographs of the device, its location, and the surrounding area should also be taken. If possible, the victim should record the time, the device’s serial number, the operator’s name, and any security cameras near the accident point. This evidence is important because the injured person must show that the device was parked in an undesignated zone in a dangerous way.

The second step is accident reporting. The victim should report the accident through the company’s operating app or customer-service center and, if necessary, also to the local government or police. The company already has information about the user who rented the device and the time when its use ended. In many cases, the return location can also be checked. Therefore, user records, trip records and return photos should be kept after an accident report. Leaving these records can make it harder for a user to remain unidentified or avoid responsibility for their mistake just because the device was shared.

The third step is compensation claiming. The victim submits medical receipts, photos, a simple explanation of the accident and the evidence they have collected to the operating company. The company should be given a certain investigation period, such as thirty days. If the investigation identifies the last user and finds that the user was at fault, the medical costs should be paid by that user. However, there are also cases where responsibility occurs for the company. If the company received a report that the sidewalk was blocked but did not move the device within a reasonable time, it should share the responsibility. In the case where the company has the technology to check the user but cannot check who the user was, the company should also take responsibility.

If agreement between the company and the victim is not made, the case should be linked to a simple procedure for dispute solving. Evidence review can be done at a court dealing with small claims, a consumer dispute mediation institution or a committee in charge of compensation for shared vehicles. Preparing this process is more realistic than requiring all victims to file a full civil lawsuit. Even if the injury is minor, medical costs, time when the victim could not work and stress can be a large burden. Therefore, the process should be made simple so that ordinary citizens can actually request compensation.

### Procedure for Work-Related Losses

Among the damages caused by illegal parking, there is also financial damage. Let’s show the one example, a situation could be thought of where a worker is driving for an important appointment with a client but cannot pass through a narrow alley because a shared bicycle is illegally parked there. The worker has to stop, reverse or find another road, and becomes late during this process. The situation can then continue to the client cancelling the meeting, the company losing a contract and the worker being dismissed. In this case, the damage is a financial loss related to work, rather than a physical injury.

At present, a special procedure applying to this situation is not well known. Making a civil claim under general tort law is possible in theory, but the process of proving it is difficult. This is because the worker needs to show the relation that the illegally parked bicycle made the delay, the delay made the worker miss the meeting, and through this, a loss that can be checked as an amount of money occurred. The dismissal also needs to be shown as connected to this loss, not to other problems inside the workplace. Therefore, a clear process for checking these connections is needed.

The procedure for this can be organized as follows. First, the victim secures evidence right after the incident. Here, photos of the blocked road, navigation records with the time marked, messages about the appointment and communications exchanged with the client or employer can be included. Afterwards, the victim makes a compensation claim to the operating company with an explanation of which device blocked the passage and how the delay occurred because of it. Lastly, an independent institution, such as a dispute mediation institution or a court handling small claims, judges the validity of the claim.

However, receiving compensation should not be made possible just with the fact that the person was late. The person making the claim should also have acted reasonably. For example, they should have allowed enough travel time, tried to contact the client and considered another route if possible. Several factors can influence a dismissal, so the compensation range should also be limited to the loss clearly caused by the obstruction. For example, if there is a specific loss, such as a commission the worker did not receive, it would be easier to calculate than the total value of future employment. Through this, actual victims can be protected while exaggerated claims can also be reduced.

## Conclusion
{: .cw2-apa-heading}

Illegally parked shared bicycles and kickboards have become a serious problem affecting pedestrian safety. Citizens’ experiences of almost falling, finding sidewalks blocked and facing dangerous situations at night show that this problem is connected to daily life (Han & Kim, 2020). Cities in Korea have responded through fines, towing, designated parking areas and an increase in collection workers. However, from the point that complaints continue to increase, these preventive measures alone can be considered insufficient (Lee, 2024; Moon, 2026).

Therefore, a clear compensation system should be prepared together with preventive measures. The aim is to let victims record the accident, report it to the operating company and then claim medical costs through a simple process. Companies should keep user and trip records and identify the responsible user where possible. If they did not move a reported hazard, they should also share the responsibility. For work-related losses, whether compensation should be given must be judged after checking the connection between the illegally parked device, the delay and the financial loss through evidence.

In future research, it is necessary to check how often these devices cause injuries to pedestrians and what kinds of injuries happen most often. It should also examine whether requiring operating companies to have insurance can make compensation simpler, and whether accident reports through apps and return photos can be used as reliable evidence. This research can help prepare a system that goes further than moving abandoned devices and protects the people who use the same sidewalks every day.
