## When Protection Becomes a Privacy Problem

Have you ever wondered who might watch you through CCTV? It is self-evident that, in this modern society, CCTV is an indispensable tool for public safety. This technology helps gather evidence in accidents, prevent crimes, and even identify or arrest criminals. Yet the information collected from CCTVs and other image surveillance tools, such as street-view services, is not always safe. This issue stems from the following facts: (a) anyone can access the recordings without permission; (b) there is a possibility of misusing personal and/or biometric information; (c) the locations of certain government facilities are sensitive and should not be publicly disclosed. Further, unlike information people voluntarily provide when signing up for services based on their needs, images may be collected from individuals who have little control over when or how they are recorded. This essay will discuss and classify the risks of image surveillance according to the three aspects where image data can violate its intended purpose: the unauthorized access to recorded footage, the misuse of personal and biometric information, and the disclosure of sensitive locations related to certain government facilities. Based on these categories, this essay argues that image surveillance should not be used as freely as it is. The use of the technology must be limited to clear scopes such as public safety and crime prevention, and the misuse beyond those scopes must be restricted.

## Unauthorized Access to Recorded Footage and Personal Privacy

The first hidden cost of video surveillance is the unauthorized access to recorded footage. Some cameras are set to provide live streams to the public, while other cameras can be accessed by hackers when organizations fail to protect recorded footage. The 2021 Verkada security breach, which affected 97 customer organizations, is a good example of how even established governments can fail to protect their citizen’s recorded footage (Verkada, Inc., 2021). A study by Li et al. (2022) on a home security camera service also showed that camera data can expose the family’s daily habits as well as when they leave home. Continuous recordings of public spaces may similarly reveal the patterns of people's movements and daily activities. These researches implicate that organizations need to curb an unauthorized person’s access to recorded footage so as to protect their citizen’s privacy, although the CCTV technology is useful for public safety.

## Biometric Misuse: Misidentification and Identity Theft

The second hidden cost is that personal and biometric data collected by cameras can be used in the wrong way. Cameras often collect data of facial recognition. Generally speaking, it helps to identify a criminal suspect or to push the clock-in button in businesses. However, biometric identification systems, including facial and gait recognition, are not always accurate and can mistakenly identify an innocent person. When the results are not accurate, an innocent person can be falsely accused, and a 2025 review demonstrates how identifying a person through facial recognition can lead to wrongful arrests and psychological trauma (Jones, 2025). Since biometric identifiers, such as facial features, are difficult to change once compromised, collecting or using them without a legitimate basis can create serious risks. Simultaneously, a malicious abuser can use the data to impersonate another person. The Federal Bureau of Investigation (FBI, 2025\) recently warned of North Korean IT workers using artificial intelligence and face-swapping technology to hide their identities during online job interviews. Such cases show that personal and biometric information can be used to create a false identity. A report by Microsoft Threat Intelligence (2025) said that, in several cases, manipulated photographs and personal documents had been used to fabricate false professional identities. Although CCTV footage had not been used in these cases, the hackers illustrate how visual information can be used for financial and political gain. If malicious actors gained access to facial images from compromised cameras along with other personal information, the North Korean hackers could potentially misuse these materials to impersonate South Korean job applicants when applying to foreign companies. Therefore, protecting a video means protecting even the identification information contained within it.

## Exposure of Sensitive Locations and National Security

The third hidden cost is the exposure of important locations. CCTVs and other image-based services can expose building entrances, nearby roads, activity patterns, etc. These can be sensitive around military installations, government offices, and critical infrastructure. When considering the security context of South Korea, this issue deserves particular attention. The Associated Press (2026) mentioned that South Korea approved exporting detailed map data for Google services. Yet there were conditions to protect sensitive information, such as military facilities, which can be viewed via Google Earth and Street View. Though Street View is not CCTV in the conventional sense, it is another form of image-based data collection that can reveal more about physical spaces than viewers might expect. If a building in the image is blurred, some information may be obscured, but nearby roads and structures can provide subsidiary context. In some cases, the presence of blurred areas may also draw attention to locations that require special protection. Hostile organizations can use this information and other sources to investigate sensitive locations. Of course, not all public visual information is at national security risk. Nonetheless, visual information related to critical locations should be evaluated with caution, as it could potentially affect the entire country.

## Conclusion: Find the Balance Between Security and Privacy

There are risks of image surveillance, including the risk of unauthorized access to recorded images and other personal data, as well as misuse of biometric data and the risk of revealing sensitive locations and activities. Eliminating image surveillance is not practical, given the valuable contributions it can make to public safety, crime prevention, and the investigation of individual crimes. Thus, instead of trying to prevent the collection of data, organizations should concentrate on responsible management of already collected information. This means strong passwords and encryption, secure camera networks, and clear rules for storing and distributing recordings. Biometric information must be collected on the basis of a valid legal basis. The identification results must be used as evidence and must not be used to decide on the guilt of a person. Additionally, images of sensitive locations, such as military facilities, should be carefully reviewed before public disclosure. In addition, it has to be used only for purposes of value that contribute to public safety, crime prevention, or individual criminal investigations, and the information collected must be used responsibly. Ultimately, the effectiveness of image surveillance depends not only on the number of cameras installed but also on how responsibly the collected information is utilized. Future research should deeply examine whether or not these measures can reduce the misuse of surveillance data while protecting individual privacy and national security.

*Risks of Image Surveillance and the Proposed Measures*

- Unauthorized viewing of recordings → Limit access, strengthen passwords and encryption, control the camera network, and set rules for storing and sharing images
- Misuse of personal and biometric information → Collect biometric data only with a legitimate legal basis, and use it to support investigations, not decide them
- Exposure of sensitive locations → Leave sensitive areas out of filming routes, or have the authorities review images before they are posted
